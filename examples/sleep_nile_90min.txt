# The Star That Opens the Year
[IMG: the night sky over the Nile valley before dawn, one bright star low on the eastern horizon, palm trees in silhouette, a calm dark river]
Settle in, and let your breathing slow down. Tonight we travel very far, more than three thousand years back in time, to a small village on the banks of the Nile in ancient Egypt. The year is around twelve hundred and fifty before our era. Far to the north, in a grand new city in the delta, the pharaoh Ramesses the Second sits on his throne. But we will not visit palaces tonight. We will stay in a quiet village near Thebes, and follow one ordinary family through one whole year.

The family you are about to meet is imagined. But almost everything around them, the river, the seasons, the fields, the bread and the lamps, comes from what historians and archaeologists have learned about daily life in ancient Egypt. So get comfortable, and let the river carry you.

[IMG: a small ancient Egyptian mud brick house at the edge of a village before dawn, flat roof, palm trees, soft blue light, a single small lamp glowing in a doorway]
It is the hour before dawn, in the hottest part of the year. The air is still warm from yesterday. On the flat roof of a small mud brick house, a man named Nakht is lying on a reed mat, awake. Many families sleep on their roofs in summer, where the night air moves a little more freely. Beside him, his wife Meryt is still asleep, and near her, their two children, curled up under a thin sheet of linen.

Nakht is looking east. He is watching for something. Every farmer in the valley knows that at this time of year, just before sunrise, a certain star returns to the sky after a long absence. The Egyptians call it Sopdet. We call it Sirius, the brightest star of the night.

[IMG: a bright blue white star rising just above the eastern desert hills in the pale pre dawn sky, a thin line of light on the horizon, silhouettes of palm trees]
For many weeks, Sopdet has been hidden, lost in the glare of the sun. But now, on one quiet morning each year, it appears again, low and faint on the horizon, just before the sun comes up. The Egyptians noticed, long ago, that this return of the star happened at almost the same time as something even more important. The rising of the river.

Nakht sees it. A small point of light, trembling above the eastern hills. He smiles to himself and does not wake anyone. The star is back. That means the new year is near, and the great flood is coming.

[IMG: the wide calm Nile river at sunrise, low water, sandbanks visible, reeds along the shore, soft golden light on the water]
Below the village, the river is at its lowest. For months it has been shrinking, pulling back from the fields, leaving sandbanks and cracked mud along its edges. The land is dry and dusty. The fields have been harvested, and their stubble has turned pale in the sun. Everything is waiting.

The Egyptians did not have a word for a year that began in winter, the way many of us do. Their year began with the water. They divided it into three seasons, each four months long. The first season was Akhet, the time of the flood. The second was Peret, the time when the fields emerged from the water and the crops grew. And the third was Shemu, the time of harvest and low water. Three seasons, twelve months of thirty days, and five extra days at the end, to bring the count close to the true year.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian village waking up at dawn, women carrying clay water jars on their heads along a dusty path, palm trees, mud brick houses, warm light]
As the sky turns from blue to gold, the village begins to stir. Somewhere a donkey complains. A rooster, or perhaps a goose, calls out. Women walk down toward the river with tall clay jars to fetch water for the day, the jars balanced carefully on their heads or shoulders. Smoke rises from a few small ovens where yesterday's embers are being fed.

Meryt wakes and sits up. She looks at her husband and sees his face. She does not need to ask. She knows he has seen the star.

[IMG: a mother and father sitting on a flat rooftop at sunrise in ancient Egypt, seen from behind, looking out over fields toward the Nile, two sleeping children on reed mats]
The two of them sit together for a moment on the roof, watching the light spread across the valley. To the east, beyond the river, the sun climbs over the desert. To the west, the cliffs of the Theban hills glow orange, and somewhere in those cliffs, far away and out of sight, lie the tombs of kings.

Between the two deserts runs the narrow green ribbon of the valley, and through its middle, the river. The Egyptians called their fertile land Kemet, the black land, after the dark rich soil the river left behind. The desert that surrounded it on both sides they called Deshret, the red land. The difference between black and red, between life and emptiness, was often only a few steps.

[IMG: the clear border between green fields and red desert sand in ancient Egypt, a narrow strip of farmland beside the Nile, cliffs in the distance, morning light]
You could stand with one foot on green, watered ground and the other on hot, bare sand. Everything Egyptians ate, wore and built depended on that thin line, and on the river that drew it.

And so, the whole valley waits for the water. Soon the river will begin to rise, slowly at first, then faster, turning brown and then reddish with mud carried from far away. No one in the village knows where that water truly comes from. They only know that it comes, every year, as it always has, and that it brings life with it.

[IMG: a small clay oil lamp glowing on a wooden table inside an ancient Egyptian house, bread and a clay jug nearby, soft warm light, peaceful]
Down in the house, Meryt lights a small clay lamp, even though the morning is bright. It is a small habit, a way of greeting the new day. She sets out bread from yesterday and a jug of water. The children stretch, yawn, and climb down the wooden ladder from the roof, still half asleep.

The day will be long and hot, like all the days of this season. But tonight, and for many nights to come, the family will watch the river. And we will watch it with them.

# The River Rises
[IMG: the Nile river beginning to flood, brown reddish water spreading slowly over low fields, palm trees standing in shallow water, soft morning light]
A few weeks pass. The heat is heavy and the air is thick with dust. Then one morning, the children run up from the riverbank shouting. The water has changed color. It is no longer clear and green, but a cloudy brown, and it has crept a little higher up the bank during the night.

This is the beginning of the inundation. Thousands of kilometers to the south, far beyond Egypt, summer rains have been falling on distant highlands. That water gathers into rivers and flows north, carrying with it enormous amounts of fine dark silt. By the time it reaches the village, the river is swollen and heavy with mud.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian farmer standing on the riverbank at dawn, seen from behind, looking at the rising muddy Nile, reeds bending in the current]
Nakht walks down to the bank and stands there for a long time, looking at the water. Every farmer asks the same quiet question at this time of year. How high will the river rise? If the flood is too low, some fields will stay dry, and there may not be enough grain next year. If the flood is too high, it can wash away dikes and villages. A good year is a flood in between, generous but not violent.

The Egyptians measured the rising water with great care. Along the river, at important places, they built structures that historians call nilometers. Some were staircases leading down into the water, with marks cut into the stone walls. Others were deep wells connected to the river. Priests and officials watched the level each day and wrote it down. From those measurements, they could guess how good the coming harvest might be.

[IMG: stone steps leading down into the Nile river, measuring marks carved into the stone wall, water rising over the lower steps, early morning, peaceful]
In our village there is no great stone nilometer, only an old wooden post driven into the mud near the landing place, with notches cut into it by some grandfather long ago. Each morning, one of the older men walks down and checks where the water has reached, then calls out the news. Another notch. And another.

The Egyptians gave the flood a divine face. They imagined it as Hapi, a gentle, round bellied god with a crown of water plants, who carried offerings of food in his arms. Hapi was not a king of the gods. He was more like a generous neighbor, bringing plenty to everyone. People sang hymns to him, thanking him for making the fields green.

[IMG: a painted wall in an ancient Egyptian temple showing a round bellied river god holding plants and offerings, soft lamplight, warm colors, calm atmosphere]
Day by day, the water rises. The low fields nearest the river disappear first. Then the canals fill and overflow. Small channels that were dry and dusty a month ago become streams. The river spreads out into the land like a slow, patient hand.

The villages of the valley were built on slightly higher ground, on natural mounds and ridges, so that when the water came they became little islands. Earth dikes connected them, raised paths that people could walk along above the flood. In a good year, the whole valley turned into a great, shining lake, dotted with villages and palm trees.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian village on a small mound surrounded by floodwater, mud brick houses, palm trees reflected in still water, soft pink evening light]
Imagine standing on the edge of the village in the evening, when the flood is near its height. All around you is water, calm and wide, reflecting the pink and orange sky. Tops of palm trees rise out of it. Far away, another village sits on its own little island, and you can see the thin threads of smoke from its cooking fires.

The noise of daily life is softer now. There are no oxen pulling ploughs, no shouting across the fields. Only frogs, and birds, and the gentle lapping of water against the mud walls of the dikes.

[IMG: a small reed boat gliding across calm flood water between palm trees at dusk in ancient Egypt, a lone figure with a pole, soft reflections]
With the fields under water, the easiest way to travel is by boat. Small light boats made of bundled papyrus reeds glide from village to village. People visit relatives, carry goods, or simply drift in the cool of the evening. The children of our village love this time of year. They splash in the shallows near the houses, and chase each other along the tops of the dikes.

Nakht's son, a boy of about nine, has been given a small task. Each afternoon he must check the family's animals, a pair of oxen, a few goats and a noisy group of geese, all moved to a pen on higher ground. He takes his job very seriously, and counts the geese twice every day.

[IMG: a young ancient Egyptian boy seen from behind counting a small flock of white geese in a pen on a mound, flood water beyond, afternoon light]
His sister, a little younger, prefers to sit by the water with her mother, watching the reflections, and sometimes helping to rinse linen in the shallows. The water is warm and brown and slow.

As the weeks go by, the river brings something more precious than water. When the flood finally settles and begins to go down, it will leave behind a thin new layer of dark silt across every field. This mud is full of nutrients. It renews the soil each year, so that the same fields can be farmed again and again, generation after generation, without becoming exhausted. The Egyptians did not need to understand why. They only knew that the black mud meant life.

[IMG: calm floodwater covering fields at sunset in ancient Egypt, a thin line of palm trees, distant cliffs glowing orange, a peaceful wide landscape]
In the evening, the family sits outside on the roof again. The air above the water is a little cooler than before. Meryt says that this looks like a good flood. Not too high, not too low. Nakht agrees. The notches on the old post are right where they should be.

They listen to the frogs. They watch the stars appear, one by one, reflected in the water below. And slowly, the village falls asleep, surrounded by the quiet river.

# The Festival of Opet
[IMG: a crowd gathering along the banks of the Nile at Thebes during a festival in ancient Egypt, colorful linen, palm trees, temple gateways in the distance, morning light]
In the second month of the flood season, when the water is high and the fields cannot be worked, the great city of Thebes holds one of its most beloved celebrations. It is called the Festival of Opet. For our family, it is the most exciting event of the whole year.

Thebes, which the Egyptians called Waset, is only a short journey from the village. Early in the morning, the family puts on their cleanest linen. Meryt lines her eyes and her daughter's eyes with dark kohl. This eye paint was used by men and women alike. It was beautiful, but people also believed it protected the eyes from the glare of the sun and from flies.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian mother applying dark kohl eyeliner to her young daughter's eyes by a doorway, soft morning light, clay pots, calm and gentle]
They travel by boat, along with many neighbors, joining a stream of little vessels all heading toward the city. The river is wide and full, and the boats move easily with the current. On both banks, crowds are gathering. There is music already, the rattle of sistrums, the beat of drums, and people singing.

At the heart of the festival is a journey. In the great temple of Karnak lives Amun, the king of the gods, together with his wife Mut and their son Khonsu. Their statues rest in the temple's innermost rooms, hidden from ordinary people for most of the year. But during Opet, the statues are placed inside small golden boats, called barques, and carried out into the light.

[IMG: priests in white linen carrying a small golden ceremonial boat on long poles on their shoulders through a huge temple gateway, sunlight, incense smoke, distant view]
Priests in white linen lift the barques onto their shoulders, using long poles. Slowly, carefully, they carry the gods out through the great gateways of Karnak and toward the temple of Luxor, a few kilometers to the south. Depending on the period, the journey was made along a road lined with sphinxes, or on the river itself, aboard great ceremonial ships.

For ordinary people, this is a rare chance to be near the gods. The procession passes close to the crowds. People bow, wave palm branches, and call out prayers. Some ask questions of the god, hoping for a sign. Musicians and dancers accompany the procession, and acrobats perform along the way.

[IMG: acrobats and dancers performing along a wide avenue in ancient Thebes during a festival, musicians with harps and drums, crowds watching, warm afternoon light]
Our family finds a place along the riverbank, under the shade of a palm tree. The children climb onto their father's shoulders to see. The little girl has never seen anything so bright. The golden barques flash in the sunlight. Clouds of incense drift across the water. The sound of hundreds of voices rises and falls like waves.

The festival is also a time of generosity. Temples distributed food and drink to the crowds during great feasts, bread, beer, and sometimes meat, things ordinary families did not always have. Meryt receives a few round loaves and a small jar of beer, and the family sits together on the grass to eat and watch.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian family sitting together on the grass by the Nile, eating round bread, a clay jar of beer, festival crowds in the background, gentle light]
Beer in ancient Egypt was not quite like modern beer. It was thick, nourishing and mild, made from barley and bread, and people of every age drank it daily as part of their meals. At festivals, there was more of it, and the mood was merry.

The festival lasts for many days. At its longest, during this period, it stretched to more than three weeks. Most villagers cannot stay for all of it. But for one long, bright day, our family forgets about fields and floods and simply enjoys the music, the crowds and the shining gods on their golden boats.

[IMG: the great temple of Luxor at dusk in ancient Egypt, lit by torches and lamps, reflections in the Nile, boats on the water, peaceful festive atmosphere]
In the evening, as the sun sets behind the western hills, lamps and torches are lit along the temple walls. The river is covered with boats heading home, each one carrying a little light. The children are tired now. The girl falls asleep against her mother's shoulder before the boat has even left the city.

The water is calm. Oars dip slowly. Someone at the back of the boat hums a song from the festival, softly, over and over. And the lights of Thebes grow smaller and smaller behind them, until they are just a warm glow on the horizon.

# Months of Water and Work
[IMG: an ancient Egyptian village surrounded by calm floodwater in late morning, people working quietly outside their mud brick houses, palm trees, soft light]
After the festival, the flood reaches its height and then rests there, wide and still, for many weeks. The fields are hidden under water. There is no ploughing to do, no sowing, no harvest. For the farmers of the valley, this is a very different kind of season.

It would be easy to imagine that everyone simply rested. And some rest did happen. But the months of the flood were also a time for all the work that could not be done while the fields demanded attention. Tools had to be repaired. Houses needed new plaster. Baskets, ropes and mats had to be made for the coming year.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian man sitting in the shade of a doorway repairing a wooden plough, tools on the ground, a goat nearby, calm afternoon light]
Nakht spends a whole morning sitting in the shade by the door, repairing the family's wooden plough. The blade has been worn down by last year's work. He carves and shapes a new piece of wood, testing its angle again and again with his hand. It is slow, careful work, and he does not hurry.

The flood season was also the time when the state could call on the labor of farmers. Egyptian kings organized great building projects, temples, canals, and monuments, and for these they needed many workers. Men from the villages could be called to serve for a period of time, cutting and hauling stone, moving earth, or repairing dikes and canals. Historians call this kind of required labor a corvée.

[IMG: a large group of ancient Egyptian workers seen from a distance hauling a block of stone on a wooden sledge along a ramp near a temple, dust in the air, warm light]
This year, Nakht is lucky. His name has not been called. But several men from the village have gone north for a few weeks, to help repair a canal. Their families wait for their return, and the neighbors help each other with the daily tasks in the meantime.

In many houses, the quietest and steadiest sound of this season is the soft turning of a spindle. Meryt sits on a low stool in the doorway, drawing out thread from a bundle of flax fibers. She twists it with a small wooden spindle that spins and drops toward the floor, then winds the finished thread around it. Her hands move without her having to think about them. She has done this since she was a child.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian woman sitting on a low stool spinning flax thread with a wooden spindle in a sunlit doorway, a basket of fibers beside her, peaceful]
Linen was the fabric of ancient Egypt. It was made from flax, a plant with slender stems and small blue flowers. After harvest, the stems were soaked, beaten and combed to separate the long fibers inside. Then those fibers were spun into thread and woven into cloth. Almost everyone wore linen, from farmers to priests to kings. The finest linen was so thin and delicate that you could almost see through it.

In our village, most of the spinning and weaving is done at home. Meryt's loom is a simple frame laid flat on the ground, with the threads stretched between two wooden beams. When she has spun enough thread, she and her neighbor will sit together and weave, passing the thread back and forth, talking quietly, while the children play nearby.

[IMG: two ancient Egyptian women weaving linen together on a horizontal ground loom in a shaded courtyard, threads stretched between wooden beams, soft light]
The cloth they make will become tunics, sheets, and wraps for the family. Some of it may be traded. Linen was valuable, and a good length of cloth could be exchanged for grain, oil, or a new clay pot.

Across the lane, a neighbor is making pottery. He sits beside a low turntable, shaping a lump of wet Nile clay with his hands. The river provides everything he needs, the mud, the water, even the reeds used to fire his small kiln. His pots are plain and practical, red brown in color, made for storing grain, carrying water, and brewing beer. Every house in the village has dozens of them.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian potter shaping a clay jar on a low turntable outdoors, rows of drying red brown pots on the ground, a small kiln behind him, afternoon sun]
Some of the pots are small and round, some tall and narrow. When they break, as pots always do, the pieces are not simply thrown away. Broken pottery was used everywhere, to scoop, to scrape, to patch, and sometimes, as we will see later, even as a kind of notepaper.

In the afternoons, when the heat is strongest, the village grows quiet. People lie down in the shade, inside their thick walled houses where the air stays cooler. Mud brick is a good material for a hot country. The walls are thick, and they hold back the heat of the day, releasing it slowly in the night.

[IMG: the cool shaded interior of an ancient Egyptian mud brick house at midday, thick walls, a small high window letting in a beam of light, reed mats on the floor]
Inside, the light is soft and dim. Small windows are placed high on the walls, letting in a little light and air while keeping out the sun. The floor is beaten earth, covered with reed mats. There is not much furniture. A few low stools, a small wooden chest for linen, perhaps a bed frame with a woven rope surface. Most people sat and slept close to the floor.

The children are supposed to rest in the afternoon, but the boy is whispering to his sister, telling her about a large fish he claims to have seen near the dike. She does not believe him. He insists. Their voices grow softer and softer, until, finally, they are both asleep.

[IMG: two ancient Egyptian children asleep on a reed mat in a shaded room at midday, a wooden toy on the floor beside them, soft light from a small window]
Outside, the flood lies still around the village. A heron stands motionless in the shallows, waiting. Far away, a boat moves slowly across the water, its single sail barely filled by the gentle breeze. And the long, warm afternoon passes, slowly, without anyone needing to do anything at all.

When the sun begins to lower, the village wakes again. Women go to fetch water. Men check their animals. Someone lights an oven, and the smell of baking bread drifts along the lane. The work of the flood season continues, day after day, patient and unhurried, like the river itself.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian village lane at golden hour, smoke from small bread ovens, women carrying water jars, a dog lying in the dust, palm trees, warm glow]
Days blend into each other. Thread becomes cloth. Clay becomes jars. The plough is mended and set against the wall, ready. And all the while, the water around the village is changing, very slightly, in a way you would only notice if you watched it closely every day. The notches on the old post have stopped rising. The river is resting at its height, and soon, it will begin to fall.

# Evenings by the Lamp
[IMG: an ancient Egyptian family preparing an evening meal in a small courtyard, a clay bread oven glowing, a woman kneading dough, a child watching, warm dusk light]
As the sun goes down, the whole village turns toward home. This is the gentlest time of the day. The heat eases. The light turns gold, then pink, then blue. And in every house, the evening meal is being prepared.

In our family's small courtyard, Meryt is making bread. She has ground grain earlier in the day, kneeling at a stone grinding slab, pushing a smaller stone back and forth over the kernels until they became flour. It is tiring work, and it takes time. Now she mixes the flour with water and a little leftover dough from yesterday, which helps the new bread rise.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian woman kneeling at a stone grinding slab grinding grain into flour, baskets of grain nearby, soft evening light in a courtyard]
Bread was the heart of every Egyptian meal. There were many kinds, round loaves, flat loaves, loaves shaped like cones, some sweetened with dates or honey, some flavored with seeds. Bread was so important that it was used as a kind of payment. Workers were often paid in bread and beer, along with other goods.

The dough goes into clay molds, or is shaped by hand and pressed against the hot inner wall of a small round oven. Soon, the smell of fresh bread fills the courtyard. The children, who have been playing nearby, suddenly appear, drawn by the smell as if by magic.

[IMG: freshly baked round loaves of bread on a woven mat beside a small clay oven in an ancient Egyptian courtyard, a jug and onions nearby, glowing light]
Dinner is simple, but good. Fresh bread. Onions and garlic, which Egyptians loved. A few lentils cooked with herbs. Some dried fish, caught by the boy's uncle and salted in the sun. Perhaps cucumbers, or lettuce from a small garden patch. Figs or dates for something sweet. And, of course, beer, thick and mild, poured from a clay jar.

Meat was not an everyday food for ordinary families. Cattle were valuable and were more often eaten at festivals or by wealthy households. But birds were plentiful in the marshes, and fish filled the river, so there was usually something to go with the bread.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian family sitting on reed mats eating dinner together in the evening, bread, onions, fish and dates on a low table, a small lamp glowing, warm and cozy]
The family eats together, sitting on mats, sharing from bowls in the middle. The boy tells everyone again about the enormous fish. This time, it has grown even larger. His father laughs and says he will believe it when he sees it on the table. The girl giggles. Meryt tears a loaf in half and gives each child a piece.

As the sky darkens, Meryt lights the lamp. It is a small, shallow clay bowl, filled with oil, with a twisted wick of linen resting at its edge. The flame is small and steady, and it throws a warm, trembling light across the walls. Lamps like this lit Egyptian homes for thousands of years.

[IMG: a small shallow clay oil lamp with a glowing flame on a low wooden stool in a dark room, soft warm light on mud brick walls, very calm]
After the meal, Nakht brings out a flat wooden box. The children cheer quietly. It is the game board. On its top is a grid of thirty squares, arranged in three rows of ten. The game is called senet, and it is one of the oldest board games we know of. Egyptians played it for well over two thousand years, and boards have been found in houses and in tombs alike.

Each player has a set of small pieces, like little cones and spools. Instead of dice, they toss four flat sticks, each light on one side and dark on the other, and count how many land light side up. Then they move their pieces along the board, trying to be the first to guide all of them to the end. Historians are not completely sure of the exact rules, but they know the game involved luck, strategy, and quite a lot of friendly teasing.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian father and son playing the board game senet by lamplight, a wooden board with squares and small game pieces, throwing sticks, cozy evening scene]
Tonight, the boy plays against his father, and his sister is allowed to throw the sticks. The lamp flickers. The pieces click softly against the wooden board. The boy thinks very hard about each move, his tongue sticking out slightly in concentration. His father, perhaps on purpose, makes a small mistake near the end, and the boy wins. He is very proud.

Meanwhile, the little girl has taken out her favorite toy, a small wooden doll with a body carved from a flat piece of wood and hair made of strings of clay beads. Egyptian children had many toys like ours, dolls, balls made of leather or linen stuffed with straw, spinning tops, and little wooden animals, some with jaws that could open and close when you pulled a string.

[IMG: a little ancient Egyptian girl sitting by lamplight holding a simple wooden doll with bead hair, a small leather ball and a wooden toy animal beside her, gentle and calm]
Outside, the village is growing quiet. From the next house comes the sound of someone singing softly, a lullaby perhaps, or a work song remembered from the fields. A dog barks once, far away, and then stops. The frogs are singing in the flooded fields, a steady, soothing chorus that goes on and on through the night.

Meryt tells the children a story. It is an old tale, the kind passed from grandmother to mother to child. Egyptians loved stories. Some that were written down have survived, tales of shipwrecked sailors on magical islands, of clever peasants who spoke so beautifully that officials wanted to keep listening, of magicians who could part the waters of a lake to find a lost jewel.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian mother telling a bedtime story to two children lying on reed mats, a small lamp glowing, shadows on the walls, peaceful night]
Tonight's story is about a sailor who is washed up on a faraway island after a storm, and who meets a great, kind serpent covered in gold, who takes care of him until a ship comes to bring him home. The children have heard it many times. They know every part. But they still ask for it, and they still listen, eyes growing heavy, as their mother's voice grows softer.

When the story ends, the girl is already asleep. The boy is trying very hard to stay awake, but his eyes close, open, and close again. Meryt covers them both with a light linen sheet.

[IMG: two ancient Egyptian children sleeping peacefully under a linen sheet on a reed mat, a clay lamp burning low beside them, soft golden light, very calm]
Nakht and Meryt sit together a little longer by the lamp. They talk quietly about ordinary things. The flood. The fields. The neighbor's new pots. Whether the canal workers will return soon. Then Meryt leans over and blows out the flame.

The room goes dark. Through the small window, a few stars are visible. The frogs keep singing. And the whole village sleeps, surrounded by water, under a sky full of stars.

# The Land Comes Back
[IMG: floodwater slowly receding from ancient Egyptian fields, dark wet mud appearing between shallow pools, palm trees, soft morning mist, calm]
Slowly, so slowly that it is hard to notice at first, the water begins to fall. One morning, the old man at the landing place calls out that the level is a little lower than yesterday. The next day, lower still. The great lake that surrounded the village begins to shrink.

This is the start of the second season, Peret, the time of emergence. As the water pulls back toward the river, the land comes back into view. First the higher ridges, then the edges of fields, then whole stretches of dark, shining mud, fresh and new, left behind by the flood.

[IMG: wide stretches of shining dark wet silt left by the Nile flood in ancient Egypt, small puddles reflecting the sky, birds walking on the mud, early morning light]
The smell of the land changes. It is rich and earthy and damp. Birds walk across the wet ground, pecking for insects and small creatures left behind by the water. Children run along the new mud, sinking to their ankles and laughing.

For the farmers, this is an important and sometimes complicated moment. The flood has covered everything, and in many places, it has washed away the markers that showed where one family's field ended and another's began. Before anyone can start ploughing, the boundaries have to be found again.

[IMG: ancient Egyptian surveyors stretching a long knotted rope across a muddy field to measure it, a scribe watching and holding a writing board, a farmer standing nearby, morning light]
This was a task for officials and scribes. Teams of surveyors came with long ropes, knotted at regular intervals, and stretched them across the fields to measure them again. Historians sometimes call these men rope stretchers. They compared their measurements with written records kept from earlier years, and restored the borders of each field.

The measurements mattered for another reason too. The size of each field, and the height of the flood, helped the state estimate how much grain each farmer should be able to produce, and therefore how much tax he would owe after the harvest. Ancient Egypt did not use coins at this time. Taxes were paid mostly in goods, especially grain.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian scribe sitting cross legged on a mat at the edge of a field writing on a papyrus scroll, a farmer standing respectfully nearby, soft light]
On the morning the surveyors come to our village, Nakht stands at the edge of his field, watching them work. The scribe with them sits on a mat, with a writing board on his knees and a reed brush in his hand, carefully noting numbers. Nakht cannot read the signs, but he knows his field well. He points out an old stone that marks one corner, half buried in the mud. The surveyors check it against their records. It is correct. The field is the same as last year.

Once the boundaries are settled, the real work begins, and it must happen quickly. The mud must not dry out too much before the seeds go in. Across the valley, everyone is out in the fields at once.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian farmer ploughing a dark muddy field with two oxen pulling a wooden plough, a helper walking alongside, morning light, flat green landscape]
Nakht brings out the plough he repaired during the flood. He yokes the two oxen to it, fixing a wooden beam across their horns or their necks. Then he walks behind them, pressing the plough down into the soft soil, guiding it in long, straight lines. In the soft wet mud, the ploughing is not as hard as it would be in dry earth. The blade slides through the ground, turning up dark soil.

In some fields, farmers did not plough at all before sowing. They simply scattered the seed onto the wet mud and let the animals trample it in. Ancient tomb paintings show exactly this. A man walks ahead, throwing seed from a bag or basket. Behind him, a flock of sheep or goats is driven across the field, pressing the seeds into the soft ground with their hooves.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian farmer scattering seeds from a basket across a muddy field, a small flock of sheep being driven behind him to trample the seeds in, soft morning light]
Nakht's son is given the job of driving the goats. He takes it very seriously, waving a thin stick and calling out to them in a stern voice. The goats do not take him quite as seriously. One wanders off to nibble at a bush, and the boy has to run after it through the mud. His father watches and smiles.

The main crops were emmer wheat and barley, the grains used for bread and beer. Farmers also sowed flax for linen, and grew vegetables like onions, leeks, lentils and chickpeas. Every seed that went into the ground in these weeks was a promise of food for the whole year to come.

[IMG: rows of freshly sown dark fields stretching toward the Nile in ancient Egypt, a few farmers working in the distance, palm trees, a calm blue sky, peaceful]
By the end of the sowing, the whole valley looks different. The great lake is gone. In its place are wide, flat fields of dark brown earth, stretching from the desert's edge down to the river, which has returned to its bed and flows calmly once more.

In the evenings, the farmers return home muddy and tired. There is a satisfied feeling in the village. The seeds are in the ground. Now there is nothing to do but wait, water, and watch.

[IMG: ancient Egyptian farmers walking home along a raised earth path at sunset, carrying tools on their shoulders, oxen walking beside them, orange sky, palm trees]
Nakht washes his feet and hands at the water jar by the door. Meryt has baked fresh bread. The children are already half asleep, exhausted from a day of running through the fields.

Tonight, when the lamp is lit and then blown out, there are fewer frogs singing. The water has gone back to the river. But the land is full of seeds, resting in the dark, waiting for the warmth of the sun to wake them.

# The Green Months
[IMG: wide green fields of young wheat and barley in ancient Egypt under a soft morning sky, the Nile calm in the distance, palm trees, a few tiny distant figures walking along a raised path]
The weeks pass slowly, and something gentle happens across the valley. The dark brown fields begin to turn green. First there is only a faint green mist over the mud, so light you might think you imagined it. Then, a few days later, there are tiny shoots, thin as threads, standing in neat rows. And then, almost without anyone noticing, the whole valley is green.

The Egyptians called this season Peret, which means something like "coming forth" or "growing". It is the season of emergence, when the plants come up out of the earth. After the drama of the flood and the hard work of the sowing, this is a quieter time. The air is cooler now. The mornings are fresh. On some nights, it is cold enough that Meryt puts an extra linen blanket over the children.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian mud brick house in the early morning, a woman seen from behind stepping out of the doorway with a clay water jar on her hip, green fields beyond, soft cool light]
Nakht still wakes before dawn. But now, instead of hurrying to the fields, he often sits a little while in the doorway with a piece of bread, watching the light come up over the eastern hills. There is less to do in the fields for a while. The crops are growing by themselves. His job now is mostly to protect them, and to make sure they have enough water.

The flood soaked the ground deeply, and for many weeks the soil stays moist underneath. But the fields that are higher up, and the gardens close to the houses, need extra water. And for that, the farmers of Nakht's time had a clever machine.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian shaduf beside a canal, a long wooden pole balanced on a post with a clay weight at one end and a bucket at the other, a farmer seen from behind lifting water, green garden beds, morning light]
It is called a shaduf. It is very simple. A long wooden pole is balanced on top of an upright post, a little like a seesaw. At one end of the pole hangs a bucket, made of leather or clay. At the other end is a heavy weight, usually a lump of dried mud or a stone. The farmer pulls the bucket down into the canal, where it fills with water. Then he lets go, and the weight on the other end lifts the heavy bucket up into the air, almost by itself. He swings it round and pours the water into a small channel that runs into the garden.

The shaduf appears in tomb paintings from this period of Egyptian history, and it is still used in some places today, more than three thousand years later. Once you have the rhythm of it, you can lift water all morning. Down, fill, up, swing, pour. Down, fill, up, swing, pour.

[IMG: a small ancient Egyptian vegetable garden divided into neat square beds by little earth walls, water flowing through tiny channels, onions and lettuce growing, a palm tree giving shade]
Nakht's garden sits just behind the house. It is divided into little square beds, each one surrounded by a low wall of earth, like a grid of small boxes. When the water comes down the channel, Nakht opens one little wall with his hand, lets the water flow into a square, and then closes it again with a push of mud, before opening the next. One square at a time, the whole garden drinks.

In these beds grow the things the family eats every day. Onions, and plenty of them. Leeks and garlic. Lettuce, which the Egyptians grew tall and upright. Cucumbers and melons. Beans, lentils and chickpeas. Near the wall, a few fig trees, and a grapevine climbing on a wooden frame, giving a little patch of shade.

[IMG: a grapevine growing over a simple wooden frame beside an ancient Egyptian house, dappled green shade, clay pots on the ground, a small cat sleeping in the shade]
The family cat likes the shade under the grapevine. Cats were loved in ancient Egypt. They kept mice and snakes away from the grain, and they appear in tomb paintings sitting under chairs, or even out in the marshes with their owners. This cat is small and sandy coloured, and it spends most of the afternoon asleep, with one ear twitching now and then.

Out in the fields, the flax is growing too. Flax is the plant that gives linen, the cloth that almost every Egyptian wore. And for a few short weeks in this season, the flax fields do something beautiful. They flower.

[IMG: a field of flax in bloom in ancient Egypt, thousands of small pale blue flowers moving gently in the breeze, green stems, the Nile and palm trees in the distance, soft morning light]
Each flax plant has small, delicate flowers, a soft pale blue. When a whole field blooms at once, it looks as if a piece of the sky has come down and settled on the earth. The flowers open in the morning and close again in the afternoon. The breeze moves across them in slow waves.

Nakht's daughter loves the flax field when it is blue. She walks along the edge of it with her mother, very carefully, not stepping on the plants, and looks at the tiny flowers up close. Meryt tells her that soon the flax will be pulled up by the roots, and soaked, and beaten, and spun into thread, and woven into cloth. The girl finds it hard to believe that her dress was once a field of little blue flowers.

[IMG: the edge of a papyrus marsh on the Nile in ancient Egypt, tall papyrus stems with round feathery tops, water birds flying up, lotus flowers floating on calm water, early morning mist]
Beyond the fields, where the land meets the river, there are the marshes. Here, tall papyrus plants grow thick along the water, much taller than a man, with feathery round heads that sway in the wind. The marshes are full of life. Ducks and geese hide among the stems. Herons stand still in the shallows. Kingfishers flash blue over the water. White and blue lotus flowers float on the quiet pools, opening in the sun.

The papyrus is useful in many ways. Its stems can be tied together in bundles to make light boats. They can be woven into mats, baskets, sandals and rope. And, of course, papyrus is famous for another use. Its inner stem can be cut into thin strips, laid in layers, pressed and dried to make sheets for writing on. But that is mostly work for special workshops. For the villagers, papyrus is mainly for boats, mats and rope.

[IMG: a small light boat made of bundled papyrus stems gliding through a green marsh in ancient Egypt, a fisherman seen from behind holding a net, water birds nearby, golden morning light]
Some mornings, Nakht takes his son out to the marsh to fish. They go in a little papyrus skiff, so light that the boy can help carry it. Nakht pushes it through the shallow water with a long pole, sliding quietly between the stems.

Egyptians caught fish with nets, with hooks and lines, with traps made of woven reeds, and sometimes with spears. Tomb paintings show fishermen pulling in big nets full of fish, and men sitting on the riverbank with fishing lines. Nakht uses a small hand net. He waits, perfectly still, watching the water. Then, in one smooth movement, he scoops. The net comes up dripping, with two silver fish flapping inside.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian father and son seen from behind sitting in a small papyrus boat in a calm marsh, the father holding up a hand net with small fish, soft sunlight through the reeds]
The boy is amazed every time. He tries it himself, and scoops only water and a few bits of weed. But he laughs, and tries again. They have all morning.

When the sun is high, they paddle back with a small basket of fish. Some will be eaten fresh tonight, grilled over the fire. Others will be split open, salted, and laid out on the roof to dry in the sun, so they can be eaten later. Dried fish was one of the most common foods in ancient Egypt, along with bread, onions and beer.

[IMG: split fish drying on a reed mat on the flat roof of an ancient Egyptian house, in bright sun, palm tree tops and green fields visible beyond, peaceful]
On the way home, they pass the green fields of barley and wheat, growing taller every day. The breeze moves across them in soft ripples, the way wind moves across water. Nakht stops at the edge of his field and runs his hand gently over the tops of the young plants. They are healthy and strong. If nothing goes wrong, if no locusts come, if no animals break in, there will be a good harvest.

He says a quiet word of thanks, and walks on. At home, Meryt is lighting the fire. The cat has moved from the grapevine to the doorway, to wait for fish. And the whole green valley rests under a pale, gentle sky.

# The Scribe of the Village
[IMG: an ancient Egyptian scribe seen from the side sitting cross-legged under a shady tree, a papyrus scroll stretched across his lap, a reed brush in hand, a wooden palette beside him, soft afternoon light]
In every village there were people who could do things that most others could not. There was the potter, who could turn a lump of river mud into a jar. There was the carpenter, who could make a bed or a box out of rough wood. And there was the scribe, who could do something that seemed almost like magic to many people. He could write.

In ancient Egypt, only a small part of the population could read and write. Historians are not sure exactly how many, but it was certainly a small minority. Most farmers, like Nakht, could not read at all. They did not need to. They knew the fields, the river, the animals and the seasons. But the country as a whole depended on writing. Someone had to measure the fields, count the grain, record the taxes and keep the lists. That someone was the scribe.

[IMG: a simple ancient Egyptian scribe's palette made of wood, with two round cakes of ink, one black and one red, and thin reed brushes resting in a slot, on a linen cloth, soft light]
The scribe in Nakht's village is a quiet, careful man. He has a small house near the centre of the village, a little larger than most, with a wooden chest full of rolled papyrus. When he goes to work, he carries his tools with him, and they are the same tools that scribes had used for more than a thousand years.

The most important is his palette. It is a long, thin piece of wood with two small hollows at one end. In one hollow is a cake of black ink, made from soot. In the other is a cake of red ink, made from red ochre, a coloured earth. Scribes wrote most things in black, and used red for headings, for important words, or for marking where a new section began. A long slot in the palette holds his brushes. They are thin stems of reed, with the ends chewed or cut so that the fibres spread a little, like a very fine paintbrush.

[IMG: a small clay water pot and a wooden scribe's palette beside a half written papyrus sheet covered in flowing black and red marks, on a reed mat, afternoon sunlight, calm]
To write, the scribe dips his brush in a little pot of water, touches it to the ink cake, and then writes with light, quick strokes. For everyday writing, scribes did not use the careful picture hieroglyphs you see carved on temple walls. They used a faster, flowing script called hieratic, which is a kind of handwriting version of hieroglyphs. On the page, it looks like a line of graceful curls and hooks, flowing from right to left.

The scribe sits cross-legged on the ground, with his linen kilt pulled tight across his knees. The kilt makes a kind of table, and he rests the papyrus on it. This is exactly how scribes are shown in ancient statues. Sitting perfectly still, back straight, head slightly bowed, looking down at the page.

[IMG: a group of young boys in ancient Egypt seen from behind sitting on the ground in the shade of a wall, practising writing on flat pieces of pottery and limestone, an older teacher sitting nearby, soft light]
Becoming a scribe took years of study. Boys, and very occasionally girls, from families that could afford it started their training young. They learned by copying. Again and again, they copied out signs, words and whole texts, until their hands knew the shapes by heart.

Papyrus was too valuable to waste on a child's practice. So students wrote on something much cheaper. They used broken pieces of pottery, and flat flakes of white limestone, which were everywhere in the Theban hills. These pieces are called ostraca. Archaeologists have found thousands and thousands of them, many covered with schoolboy exercises, some with mistakes that the teacher has corrected in red.

[IMG: a flat flake of white limestone with lines of faded black handwriting on it, lying on sandy ground, warm afternoon light, a small reed brush resting beside it]
One of the texts that students copied was a famous old composition, known today as the Satire of the Trades. In it, a father takes his son to school, and on the way he describes all the other jobs a man could do. And he makes every one of them sound terrible. The potter is covered in mud. The fisherman is afraid of crocodiles. The farmer is tired and worried all year long. Only the scribe, the father says, lives a good and comfortable life. No one tells him what to do. He is the one who gives the orders.

It is a funny text, and it was meant to encourage lazy students to keep studying. You can almost imagine the teacher reading it out loud, and a room full of boys sighing and going back to their copying.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian scribe seen from behind standing at the edge of a field, holding a long coiled measuring rope, a farmer standing respectfully nearby, green crops, a calm afternoon]
Today, the scribe has come out to Nakht's field. He is not alone. Two men carry a long rope, knotted at regular distances. This is how fields were measured. The rope is stretched along the edge of the field, and the scribe counts the lengths, and writes the numbers down.

Why measure the field? Because the size of the field, and the height of this year's flood, would help decide how much grain the farmer was expected to give as tax at harvest time. The flood had washed away the old boundary marks in some places, and new lines had to be agreed. So, every year, the scribes went out with their ropes and their palettes.

[IMG: two ancient Egyptian men seen from a distance stretching a long rope across a green field, a scribe sitting on a small stool writing on a papyrus, palm trees, gentle light]
Nakht watches carefully, a little nervous, as farmers always are when the scribe comes. But this scribe is fair, and the measurement is the same as last year. The scribe writes it down on his papyrus, blows gently on the ink to dry it, and rolls it up.

Nakht's son has come too. He is fascinated. He stands close to the scribe, watching the brush move across the page. The scribe notices, and smiles, and shows him how the sign for "water" is written. Three little zigzag lines, one above the other, like small waves. The boy traces the shape in the air with his finger.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian boy seen from behind drawing wavy lines in the dust with a stick, a scribe seated nearby watching kindly, late afternoon light, a quiet village lane]
That evening, the boy sits in the dust outside the house and draws zigzag lines with a stick. Water. Water. Water. His little sister wants to learn too, so he shows her. She draws hers too big, and they spread across the whole path, and they both laugh.

Nakht watches them from the doorway. He knows his son will most likely become a farmer, like him and his father before him. That is how life usually went. But it is nice, he thinks, to see the boy so curious.

[IMG: the interior of a simple ancient Egyptian house at night, a small clay oil lamp glowing on a shelf, rolled reed mats, clay pots along the wall, warm soft light, calm and quiet]
Later, when the lamp is lit, the house is quiet. Somewhere across the village, the scribe is probably sitting with his own lamp, adding up the day's numbers. Field by field, farm by farm, his lists grow. Many of those lists, written by scribes like him, have survived in the dry sands of Egypt. They are one of the reasons we know so much about ordinary life along the Nile.

The children fall asleep, still dreaming, perhaps, of little zigzag waves.

# The Village of the Tomb Builders
[IMG: a small walled village of mud brick houses in a dry desert valley on the west bank of Thebes, rocky cliffs behind, narrow lanes, early morning light, peaceful, no people in focus]
Across the river from Thebes, on the west bank, the green fields end suddenly at the edge of the desert. Behind them rise tall, pale cliffs of limestone. And tucked into a dry little valley among those hills, there was a very special village. Today it is known as Deir el-Medina.

This was the village of the men who built the royal tombs. For hundreds of years, the craftsmen who cut and decorated the tombs of the pharaohs in the Valley of the Kings lived here with their families. And because the village was in the dry desert, far from the damp fields, an enormous amount of what they left behind has survived.

[IMG: rows of small ancient Egyptian houses packed closely together along one narrow main street in a desert village, flat roofs, small doorways, pale cliffs rising behind, soft golden morning light]
Let us imagine that Nakht has a cousin who lives here, a stone cutter, and that one quiet day the family has crossed the river to visit him.

The village is small. A wall runs around it, and inside there are about seventy houses, packed tightly together along a narrow main street. Each house is long and narrow, with several rooms one behind the other. Many have a small shrine in the front room, and steps leading up to the flat roof. There is no river here, and no well. Water has to be carried up from the valley, by donkey, and poured into big jars. The village depends on the state for its water, its grain and its supplies.

[IMG: a line of donkeys carrying large clay water jars along a dusty desert path toward a small walled village, a man walking beside them seen from behind, morning light, pale hills]
Nakht's cousin greets them at the door with a warm embrace. He is a strong man, with hands that are scarred and hard from years of work with copper chisels and stone. His house smells of bread and dust.

He explains how his life works, and it is quite different from a farmer's. He does not grow his own food. Instead, he is paid by the state, mostly in grain. Each month, sacks of wheat and barley arrive, along with other things like fish, vegetables, oil and firewood. From this, his family eats, and anything extra can be traded for other goods.

[IMG: a narrow rocky path winding over desert hills toward a hidden valley, the Theban cliffs glowing in early sunlight, a small group of workmen seen far away walking in single file]
His work is in a hidden valley, over the hills. Every working week, the men walk the path over the ridge, and stay in small stone huts near the tombs, before coming back home to the village.

The Egyptians divided their month into three weeks of ten days each. And the tomb builders had a working pattern that sounds very familiar to us. They worked for eight days, and then they rested for two. Two days off at the end of each week. It is sometimes called the first known weekend in history.

[IMG: the dark entrance of a royal tomb cut into the rocky cliff face of a desert valley, a few distant workmen carrying baskets of stone chips, pale rock, bright morning sun]
Inside the tomb, the work was organised carefully. The crew was divided into two groups, called the left side and the right side, a little like two teams. Each group had a foreman, and a scribe kept records of everything. How many days each man worked. How much was cut. Which tools were handed out, and which were returned.

The copper chisels were precious, so they were weighed when they were given out and weighed again when they came back, to make sure nothing was lost. Even the lamp wicks were counted. Deep inside the tomb, where no sunlight could reach, the men worked by the light of small lamps, and the scribes recorded how many wicks were used each day.

[IMG: a narrow tomb corridor inside a cliff lit by small flickering oil lamps, smooth white plastered walls, a painter seen from behind carefully drawing faint red outlines on the wall, warm dim light]
First, the stone cutters carved the long corridors and rooms into the rock, chipping away at the limestone. The chips were carried out in baskets. Then the plasterers smoothed the walls. And then came the artists.

The painters worked in a careful way. First, one artist drew the outlines of the figures in red ink. Then a master looked at the drawing and made corrections in black ink, adjusting a line here, fixing a shape there. Only after that did the carvers and painters fill in the colours. In some unfinished tombs, you can still see these red and black lines today, exactly as the artists left them, as if they had just put down their brushes.

[IMG: an unfinished ancient Egyptian wall painting showing faint red sketch lines and a few black correction lines on a smooth pale wall, soft lamplight, a single brush and a small pot of paint on the floor]
The cousin tells Nakht that right now the team is working on a tomb for the pharaoh himself. It will be deep and grand, with many rooms. But it is still being cut, slowly, chip by chip, as it has been for years. A royal tomb could take many years to finish.

Nakht listens, amazed. He has never been inside a tomb like that. Very few people ever would. The work was secret, and the Valley of the Kings was guarded.

[IMG: a flat pottery shard with faded black handwriting and a small sketch of a cat lying on dusty ground in a desert village, warm evening light]
What makes Deir el-Medina so special for historians is the writing. Many of the workmen could read and write, at least a little, which was unusual for the time. And they wrote on everything. Thousands of ostraca have been found in and around the village. Letters to friends. Lists of laundry. Receipts for a bed or a coffin. Short love poems. Little sketches of animals. And notes explaining why someone did not come to work.

Those notes are wonderful. On one list of absences, men are excused because they were ill, or because they were brewing beer, or because a family member needed them. On others, a man was stung by a scorpion, or had a problem with his eyes. These little scraps of pottery let us hear the voices of ordinary workers from more than three thousand years ago.

[IMG: the flat roof of a small house in an ancient desert village at dusk, a family seen from behind sitting on reed mats, bread and a jug between them, the cliffs glowing orange, first stars appearing]
In the evening, the two families sit together on the flat roof. The cliffs above them turn orange, then pink, then grey. The children run back and forth between the roofs, playing with the cousin's children. Someone brings bread, dates and a jug of beer.

Nakht's cousin points up at the hills. Somewhere up there, he says, is the peak shaped like a pyramid that watches over the valley of the kings. A natural pyramid, made by the gods and not by men.

[IMG: a pyramid shaped mountain peak above a desert valley at twilight, deep blue sky, a few bright stars, the faint glow of a few lamps in a small village far below]
When darkness falls, the village is very quiet. There are no frogs here, no river sounds, only the soft desert wind and, now and then, a dog barking in the distance. The children sleep on the roof, tucked under linen blankets, and above them the stars are brighter than anywhere else.

Tomorrow, the family will walk back down to the river and cross to their own green fields. And the cousin will pick up his chisel and walk over the hills again, to carve a little more of a king's long, dark home.

# Market Day by the River
[IMG: a busy but calm riverside market in ancient Egypt in the morning, people seen from a distance sitting beside baskets of vegetables and fish, small boats pulled up on the bank, palm trees, soft golden light]
Now and then, there is a market by the river. Not a great city market, but a gathering on the riverbank near the village, where boats pull up and people come from the nearby farms to exchange what they have for what they need.

Meryt has been getting ready for days. She has woven a small stack of linen cloth, fine and even, from the flax the family grew last year. She has a basket of onions from the garden, and a jar of dried fish. The children are excited. Market day is noisy and colourful, and there is always something new to see.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian woman seen from behind walking along a dirt path toward the river, carrying a folded stack of linen cloth and a basket, two small children walking beside her, early morning light]
They walk down to the river in the cool of the morning. Already there are people spread out along the bank. Some sit on the ground behind low baskets. Others have set up simple shades of reed mats on poles, to keep off the sun. Tomb paintings from this period show scenes very much like this. Women and men sitting with baskets of goods, people bargaining, a monkey on a leash, and boats unloading at the water's edge.

The first thing you would notice, if you walked into this market, is that there is no money. No coins at all. Coins would not come to Egypt for many hundreds of years. Instead, people traded. A basket of fish for a pair of sandals. A length of linen for a jar of oil. A few loaves of bread for a bundle of vegetables.

[IMG: two ancient Egyptians seen from the side sitting on the ground facing each other, exchanging a pair of woven sandals for a basket of fish, a reed shade above them, the river behind, calm light]
But how do you know if a pair of sandals is worth a basket of fish? For this, the Egyptians had a clever idea. They used a common measure of value, a weight of copper called a deben. A deben was a little under a hundred grams of metal. Nobody needed to actually hand over copper. Instead, both sides would agree on how many deben their goods were worth, and then balance the exchange.

So Meryt might say, this cloth is worth so many deben. And the man with the jar of oil might say, my oil is worth a little less. And then she might add a few onions to his side, or he might add a small basket of dates, until both sides were even and everyone was satisfied. Records from Deir el-Medina show prices for all kinds of things written in deben this way. A goat, a bed, a coffin, a pair of sandals, each had its value.

[IMG: a simple set of ancient Egyptian balance scales with a few small copper weights beside baskets of grain on a riverbank market, warm sunlight, calm]
Meryt is good at bargaining. She is patient, and she knows the value of her cloth. She walks slowly along the bank, looking at everything, before she decides what she wants. Today, she needs a new cooking pot, some oil for the lamps, and, if possible, a little honey for the children as a treat.

The children stay close, but their eyes are everywhere. There is a man selling small wooden toys. There is a woman with a basket of fresh figs. There is a potter with a whole row of clay jars, from tiny ones no bigger than a fist to great big storage jars that come up to the boy's chest.

[IMG: a row of ancient Egyptian clay pots and jars of different sizes on the ground at a riverside market, red brown pottery, a reed shade overhead, soft shadows, peaceful]
While Meryt talks with the potter, the children wander to the water's edge to watch the boats. The Nile was the great road of ancient Egypt. There were very few proper roads between towns, and no one travelled far by land if they could help it. Everything moved on the river. Grain, stone, cattle, people, letters, news. All of it floated on the Nile.

And here there is something lovely about the river. In Egypt, the wind usually blows from the north, toward the south. But the river flows the other way, from the south toward the north, down to the sea. So if you want to travel south, you raise your sail, and the wind pushes you up the river. And if you want to travel north, you lower the sail, and simply let the current carry you downstream.

[IMG: a wooden sailing boat with a tall white rectangular sail travelling up the Nile in ancient Egypt, green banks and palm trees on either side, gentle blue sky, soft light]
The Egyptians were so used to this that it even shaped their writing. The hieroglyph meaning to travel south showed a boat with its sail up. And the sign meaning to travel north showed a boat with its sail down. The whole country lived by this gentle pattern of wind and water.

The children watch a big boat come in from the south, its sail folded, drifting with the current. The sailors guide it to the bank with long oars. Men begin to unload sacks and baskets, calling out to one another.

[IMG: a wooden boat without its sail drifting down the Nile with the current, sailors seen from a distance guiding it with long oars toward the riverbank, evening light on the water]
Further out on the river, a smaller boat is heading south with its white sail full of wind. It moves smoothly against the current, as if it is being pulled by an invisible rope. The boy watches it until it is just a small white shape far up the river.

He asks his mother where it is going. Meryt does not know. Perhaps to the great temples further south. Perhaps all the way to the edge of Egypt, where the river is full of rocks. The boy decides that one day he will sail a boat like that, all the way to the end of the river.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian boy seen from behind standing at the edge of the Nile, watching a distant sailing boat heading south, the sun sparkling on the water, palm trees along the far bank]
By the middle of the morning, Meryt has done well. Her linen has become a new clay pot, a small jar of oil, and a little pot of honey wrapped in a leaf. The children carry the honey home very, very carefully, as if it were made of gold.

On the way back, they share a few fresh figs that a kind woman gave them. The figs are soft and sweet. The morning is warm now. Behind them, the market is slowly packing up, and the boats are pushing off again, some drifting north, some sailing south, all moving slowly along the great river road.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian mother and two children seen from behind walking home along a raised earth path between green fields, carrying a basket and a clay pot, midday sun, palm trees, calm]
That evening, the children each get a small taste of honey on a piece of bread. Honey was precious in ancient Egypt. It came from bees kept in clay hives, and it was the main sweetener that people had. The children eat it slowly, making it last as long as they can.

Outside, the river is quiet again. The boats have gone. And the soft north wind blows over the village, as it has done for thousands of years, pushing gently toward the south.

# The Golden Harvest
[IMG: wide fields of ripe golden wheat and barley in ancient Egypt under a bright morning sky, the Nile lower in its bed in the distance, palm trees, a few distant figures at the edge of the field]
The green fields do not stay green for ever. Slowly, as the days grow warmer, the colour starts to change. First a pale yellow creeps into the tips of the plants. Then the stalks turn golden. And one morning, Nakht walks out to his field and sees that the whole thing is gold from end to end, the heavy heads of grain bending gently in the breeze.

It is harvest time. The Egyptians called this season Shemu. It was the third and last season of their year, a time of heat, of ripeness, and of a lot of hard work.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian farmer seen from behind standing at the edge of a golden wheat field, holding a wooden sickle, early morning light, the heads of grain bending gently]
Before any grain can be cut, the scribes come out again with their measuring ropes. They look at the crops and estimate how much each field will produce. This helped decide the tax that each farmer would owe. Tomb paintings from this time show the whole scene, with scribes writing, men stretching ropes across the fields, and farmers standing nearby, waiting to hear the numbers.

Then the cutting can begin. Nakht and his neighbours work together, moving slowly through the fields in a line. Each man holds a sickle. The Egyptian sickle was a curved wooden tool, and along its inner edge, set into a groove, there was a row of small, sharp pieces of flint. Flint could be chipped to an edge that cut through grain stalks easily. Some later sickles were made of metal, but wooden sickles with flint teeth were used for a very long time.

[IMG: a line of ancient Egyptian harvesters seen from a distance cutting golden wheat with curved sickles, bundles of grain on the ground behind them, bright sun, blue sky, palm trees]
The men do not cut the stalks close to the ground. They grab a handful of grain just below the heads, and cut high, leaving most of the straw standing in the field. The straw would be gathered later, for animal feed, for making mud bricks, and for many other uses.

It is hot work. As the sun climbs, the men begin to sing. Harvest songs appear written beside the paintings in some Egyptian tombs, short work songs sung by the men in the fields and by the drivers of the animals. The songs keep everyone moving together, in rhythm, one step at a time.

[IMG: ancient Egyptian women and children seen from behind collecting fallen ears of grain into small baskets in a harvested field, golden stubble, soft afternoon light, a calm scene]
Behind the men come the women and children, gathering the cut heads of grain into baskets. And behind them, following slowly, come the gleaners. These are people who pick up the ears of grain that were dropped or missed. Gleaners are shown in harvest scenes in Egyptian tombs, sometimes poorer people, sometimes children, carefully filling their small bags with what was left behind.

Nakht's daughter loves to glean. She walks through the stubble with a small basket, picking up every stray ear of wheat she can find. When she finds a whole bunch that someone has dropped, she holds it up and shouts, and everyone laughs.

[IMG: donkeys carrying large baskets of harvested grain along a dusty path in ancient Egypt, a man walking beside them seen from behind, golden fields, bright afternoon light]
The baskets of grain are loaded onto donkeys, and the donkeys carry them to the threshing floor. This is a flat, round area of hard beaten earth, near the edge of the fields. The grain is spread out across it in a thick layer.

And now comes one of the most peaceful sights of the whole year. The cattle are brought in. A few oxen or cows are driven in slow circles around the threshing floor, walking over the grain again and again. Their heavy hooves break the heads open and knock the kernels loose from the husks. A man walks behind them with a stick, guiding them and talking to them softly. Around and around they go, under the hot sun.

[IMG: a small group of oxen walking slowly in a circle over a round threshing floor covered in golden grain in ancient Egypt, a man seen from behind guiding them with a stick, dust in the warm light]
In one Egyptian tomb, a short song is written beside a scene like this, a gentle call from the farmers to the oxen, encouraging them to keep threshing, because the grain is for them and for their masters. It gives a lovely sense of how the farmers spoke to their animals, patiently, almost as friends.

When the threshing is done, there is a pile of grain mixed with broken husks and chaff. Now it must be cleaned. This is called winnowing. The workers use flat wooden scoops. They lift the grain high into the air and let it fall. The heavy grain drops straight down, but the light chaff is caught by the breeze and floats away in a soft golden cloud.

[IMG: ancient Egyptian workers seen from behind tossing grain into the air with wooden scoops on a threshing floor, golden chaff drifting away on the breeze, warm evening light]
Winnowing is best done in the late afternoon, when the breeze is steady. The air above the threshing floor fills with drifting chaff that glows in the low sunlight. The children run through it, laughing, and come out covered in tiny golden flakes.

Then the clean grain is measured. A scribe sits by the pile, and workers fill a measuring container again and again, and each time the scribe makes a mark. So much for the tax. So much for the temple. So much to keep for the family.

[IMG: a row of tall rounded ancient Egyptian granaries made of mud brick, with small openings near the top, a man seen from behind climbing a ladder to pour grain in, bright blue sky]
The family's share is carried home and stored. Egyptian granaries were often tall, rounded structures made of mud brick, with an opening near the top where the grain was poured in, and a small door near the bottom where it could be taken out. Models of granaries have been found in tombs, showing workers climbing up ladders with sacks of grain on their shoulders.

When Nakht's grain is safely stored, he stands and looks at it for a long moment. Enough bread and beer for his family for a whole year. Enough seed for next year's sowing. He feels a deep, quiet relief.

[IMG: a small ancient Egyptian household shrine with a clay figure of a cobra goddess and an offering of bread, grain and flowers in front of it, a small lamp glowing, warm soft light]
In the house, Meryt makes a small offering of the first grain to Renenutet. She was a harvest goddess, often shown as a cobra, who was believed to protect the crops and the granaries. Farmers thanked her at harvest time. Meryt places a little bowl of grain and a few flowers in front of the small shrine, and lights a lamp.

That night, everyone is exhausted. Nakht's shoulders ache. The children's hands are scratched from gleaning. But the harvest is in. The family eats fresh bread, made from new grain, and it tastes better than any bread all year.

[IMG: harvested fields of golden stubble in ancient Egypt at dusk, a few bundles of straw, the sky turning soft orange and pink, palm trees in silhouette, very calm and quiet]
Outside, the fields are bare now, just rows of short golden stubble under the evening sky. The threshing floor is quiet. The oxen are resting. And a soft, satisfied stillness settles over the whole village.

# The Long Quiet Days
[IMG: a quiet ancient Egyptian village in the heat of summer, mud brick houses with flat roofs, dry brown fields, the Nile low and calm in the distance, a hazy pale sky, stillness]
After the harvest, the year slows down. The fields are empty and dry. The ground cracks in the heat. The river is at its lowest now, a thinner, slower stream than it was months ago, with wide banks of mud and sand showing along its edges.

These are the hottest days of the year. The sun stands high and white in the sky, and in the middle of the day the whole village falls quiet. People stay inside, where the thick mud brick walls keep the rooms cool. Mud brick was a very good building material for Egypt. It kept the heat out during the day and slowly released warmth during the cooler nights.

[IMG: the cool shaded interior of an ancient Egyptian mud brick house at midday, a small high window letting in a single beam of light, a woman seen from behind resting on a reed mat, clay jars along the wall]
There is little to do in the fields now. But there is always something to do around the house. Nakht repairs his tools. He fits new flint teeth into his sickle. He mends the handle of his hoe. He fixes the rope on the shaduf. Meryt spins flax into thread with a spindle, twisting it slowly between her fingers. The long threads wind around and around, ready to be woven into cloth.

The children play in the shade. They have simple toys. A ball made of leather stuffed with straw. A small wooden animal on wheels, pulled along on a string. A board game drawn on a flat piece of wood, played with small pieces and throwing sticks. Board games were loved in ancient Egypt. One game, called senet, was so popular that boards have been found in homes and in tombs, from simple scratched boards to beautiful ones made for kings.

[IMG: two ancient Egyptian children seen from behind sitting on a reed mat playing a board game with small pieces on a wooden board, in the cool shade of a doorway, soft light]
The boy and his sister play senet in the doorway. The boy usually wins. But today his sister wins twice in a row, and she is so pleased with herself that she tells everyone in the house, and then tells the cat.

In the afternoons, when the worst of the heat has passed, the village wakes up again. Women walk down to the river to fetch water. Men sit together in the shade and talk. Old people tell stories. The long evenings are slow and golden.

[IMG: the Nile river at low water in the late afternoon in ancient Egypt, wide sandy banks, a few small boats resting on the shore, palm trees, warm golden light on calm water]
At night, it is too hot to sleep inside. So, as at the very beginning of our story, the family carries their reed mats up to the flat roof. From up here, there is a little breeze. The mud walls are still warm under their hands. And above them, the sky is enormous, full of stars.

Nakht lies on his back and looks up. The children lie beside him. His son asks him the names of the stars. Nakht knows some of them, the ones every farmer knew. The Egyptians saw pictures in the stars, like we do. They saw the foreleg of a bull, and a hippopotamus, and a figure they linked with the god Osiris in the stars we call Orion.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian family seen from behind lying on reed mats on a flat roof at night, looking up at a sky full of bright stars, palm trees in dark silhouette, very calm]
And every morning now, before dawn, Nakht wakes and looks toward the eastern horizon. He is waiting for something. For weeks, one particular star has been missing from the sky. It has been hidden, too close to the sun to be seen. But the time is coming when it will return.

The Egyptians called this star Sopdet. We know it as Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky. Its return, rising just before the sun after weeks of being hidden, was one of the most important signs of the Egyptian year. It came around the same time that the river began to rise again. For the Egyptians, it announced the coming flood, and the start of a new year.

[IMG: the eastern horizon over the Nile valley just before dawn, one single bright star shining low above dark hills, a thin glow of light beginning, palm trees in silhouette, still and quiet]
At the very end of the Egyptian year, there were five extra days. The Egyptian calendar had twelve months of thirty days each, which added up to three hundred and sixty days. The five extra days were added on at the end, to complete the year. The Egyptians said these were the birthdays of five gods. Osiris, Horus, Seth, Isis and Nephthys. They were special days, days between one year and the next.

During these days, the village is calm and waiting. The old year is almost over. The new year has not quite begun. Everyone is watching the river, and watching the eastern sky.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian man seen from behind standing alone on a flat roof before dawn, looking toward the eastern horizon where one bright star glows, the village asleep around him, deep blue sky]
And then, one morning, Nakht climbs the steps to the roof in the darkness before dawn. The air is warm and still. The village is asleep. He looks to the east, where the sky is just beginning to pale.

And there it is. Low on the horizon, just above the dark line of the hills, a single bright star, flickering softly. Sopdet has returned.

Nakht stands there quietly for a long time, watching it. He does not wake anyone. He just breathes, slowly, and watches the star shine for a few minutes before the rising sun washes it away.

[IMG: the Nile at dawn, the water beginning to rise and turn a soft reddish brown, mist over the fields, a single star fading in the pale sky, palm trees, peaceful and still]
Down by the river, the water is changing colour. Far to the south, rains have fallen in distant mountains that Nakht will never see. And now, slowly, patiently, the water is coming. In a few days, the river will begin to rise. The canals will fill. The fields will drink. And the whole great circle of the year will begin once more.

The flood. The sowing. The green months. The harvest. The long quiet days. And then the star again, and the flood again. Year after year, generation after generation, for thousands of years, the life of Egypt moved in this gentle, reliable circle.

[IMG: an ancient Egyptian mud brick house on a quiet morning, a small family seen from far behind sitting together on the flat roof, the Nile shining in the distance, the first soft golden sunlight]
Nakht climbs back down the steps. Meryt is stirring, and the children are still asleep, curled up on their mats. He sits down beside them, and lets his eyes close for a little while longer.

There is no need to hurry. The river will come when it comes. The fields will wait. The year is long, and the Nile is patient.

[IMG: the calm Nile river under a soft night sky full of stars, still water reflecting starlight, palm trees in dark silhouette along the bank, very peaceful, no people]
And now you can rest too. Let the sound of the river fill your mind. The slow water, flowing north. The soft wind, blowing south. The warm roof under a sky full of stars. Let your breathing slow down, a little more, and a little more.

The family is sleeping now, in their small house by the great river. The village is quiet. The fields are waiting. And far away on the eastern horizon, a single bright star keeps its patient watch over the long, gentle life of the Nile.

Sleep well.
